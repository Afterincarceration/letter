The regional dragnet

A camera isn't an island — it's a node on a national network.

Flock's cameras feed a searchable nationwide system. Independent audits document federal and out-of-state access to local data three ways:

Front door
Direct 1:1 data-sharing with agencies that cooperate with ICE.
Back door
Federal searches that appear in audit logs even without explicit sharing.
Side door
Local officers running searches on behalf of federal immigration agents.

That's why a single city's privacy promise can be undone by the network around it — and why which agencies your data is shared with is the question that matters.

Case study: Newburgh, NY

A "Safe and Welcoming" sanctuary city running 80+ cameras on a federally-reachable network.

  • The City paid Flock $274,400 in 2024, funded by a NYS DCJS grant; cancelling mid-contract reportedly costs ~$400,000.
  • A resolution to end the contract has been raised three times with no recorded vote.
  • Residents warn the cameras create an ICE-access pathway that contradicts the city's sanctuary policy.
The fix isn't "more cameras with promises." It's a recorded vote, public release of the data-sharing settings, and the audit logs that show who has actually searched the cameras.

Background, sources, a ready-to-send public-records (FOIL) request, and a council comment script are in the research folder on GitHub.
